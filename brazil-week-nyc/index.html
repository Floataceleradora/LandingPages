Decision frameworks for financial product builders.

Explore how different product challenges demand a different strategic approach. Two outcomes shape every framework below.

Outcome 01

Sales Adoption

Will the product earn its place in the user's life?

Adoption is not a launch event. It is the result of a financial product earning daily relevance with the customer. Sales adoption is the discipline of designing a product that fits inside the user's existing journey, captures the right to serve, and converts at unit economics that work.

Outcome 02

Operational Maturity

Will the operation work at the volume it scaled to?

An operation that ran at launch volume can break at scale volume. Operational maturity is the discipline of building the financial product so the unit economics, the vendor architecture, the regulatory perimeter, and the ownership of flow all hold up. Not just at PMF, but at the volume the operator is now running.

Symptoms you may be seeing
  • Expectations set on winning through price
  • Customer adoption below target among existing users
  • Low NPS on the new financial product
  • ROI on the embedded finance investment under board pressure
→ Sales adoption

A SaaS or marketplace that embeds finance can lift retention meaningfully, but only when the product genuinely sticks to its existing user base. Price is not where SaaS wins; banks have scaled infrastructure and funding power. The defensible play is stickiness to the core business: the new product living inside the conversations sales and CS already have with the customer. Side-businesses with separate operations stall exactly when the customer relationship is being consolidated.

The financial product lives inside boundaries the customer already trusts you with. That is what "right to serve" means.
Symptoms you may be seeing
  • Increasing friction and dissatisfaction with infrastructure vendors
  • Persistent operational bottlenecks resisting solutions
  • High cost of capital at current volume
  • EBITDA pressure as the operation scales
→ Operational maturity

The product worked. Users adopted it. Volume grew. Then the vendor stack you launched on (processors, BaaS, issuers) became the biggest cost line in the operation. Margin that should be yours goes upstream. Reclaiming it means measuring how the take-rate splits between operator and infrastructure, then insourcing where volume justifies it or renegotiating vendors priced at launch volume. Multi-BaaS structures in Brazil are getting less permissible. The stack you design now must survive that.

The operation keeps the economics it earned, instead of paying them back upstream.
Symptoms you may be seeing
  • Heavy dependency on recurring short-term loans
  • Multiple and fragmented financial vendors
  • Costly operational friction in receivables and payables
  • Channel conflict over who controls financing
→ Operational maturity

Your operation generates real financial flow: receivables from sales, payables to suppliers, financing demand inside your channel. But intermediaries sit between you and that flow, taking margin on assets you created. The pattern repeats across the real economy. A retailer leaves a piece of every installment sale to its acquirer. A distributor cannot finance its dealer network at the moment financing would accelerate sales. A hospital watches patients struggle with billing terms a captive program would solve. A university sees students drop out over installment friction. An agribusiness sees farmers underbuy inputs because credit is missing at the point of sale. The flow was strategic before it was financial.

If your operation generated the flow, the default is to own it. Not to rent it back from the chain.
Symptoms you may be seeing
  • Cost of capital limiting financial product growth
  • Strong brand and customer trust, but no banking infrastructure
  • Distribution channel with untapped embedded finance demand
  • BaaS or fintech-vendor approach hitting scale limits
→ Sales adoption + Operational maturity

When a bank and an enterprise decide to launch a financial product together (airline, retailer, insurance carrier, loyalty operator), the value is structural: capital and license from the bank, distribution and customer trust from the enterprise. The risk is also structural. Who owns the customer? Who controls the data? Who has the right to serve which segment? If those questions are not answered at structuring, channel conflict eventually breaks the partnership. The work is designing the operating governance (distribution economics, data property, right-to-serve boundaries) so each party's core business stays strong while the product scales at bank speed.

A partnership that scales at bank speed without breaking the enterprise's channel or the bank's risk model.
Symptoms you may be seeing
  • Significant overlap in customer bases between the two parties
  • Operational redundancies in day-to-day functions
  • Synergy claims driving deal valuation
  • Pricing-alignment risk for shared customers post-merger
→ Sales adoption + Operational maturity

M&A in financial products begins before deal terms. Mapping the synergies (overlapping customer bases, complementary product portfolios, shared infrastructure leverage) determines whether the transaction creates real value or only paper value. Valuation depends on which synergies are real and which are wishful. And when customer bases overlap, pricing alignment for shared customers becomes a critical operational decision. What does the customer see when two products with two different prices become one company's portfolio?

A transaction priced for the synergies that actually exist, integrated without breaking the customer experience.
